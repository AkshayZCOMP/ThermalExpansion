# Methods and Results: Physics-Structured Neural Retrieval of 10-Ply Laminate Layups

Updated: 2026-10-28

## Methods

### Problem formulation and data

The objective was to identify a ten-ply composite laminate from its thermally induced free-curvature response. Each laminate was represented as an ordered, bottom-to-top sequence of ten ply angles selected from -45, 0, +45, and 90 degrees. The response target was the area-averaged curvature vector `(kappa_x, kappa_y, kappa_xy)` generated by the nonlinear FREEFREE Foppl--von Karman solver for prescribed top- and bottom-face temperatures. The database comprised 1,024 converged solver records: 64 canonical layups evaluated at 16 temperature fields. Each sampled layup was evaluated at every field, and records were checked for convergence and duplicate layup-temperature keys.

### Machine-learning acceleration

The nonlinear equilibrium solver provides the laminate deformation, but its iterative solution is computationally expensive when many candidate layups must be considered. We therefore used a neural network as a forward surrogate rather than as a direct inverse classifier. For a measured curvature and known face temperatures, the surrogate predicts the curvature of each candidate layup; candidates are then ranked without repeatedly executing the nonlinear solver.

Each candidate layup is represented by a 10 x 4 one-hot encoding of its ply angles. For the signed face-to-face temperature difference, `Delta_Tf`, classical lamination theory (CLT) first provides a baseline curvature, `kappa_CLT`. The neural network uses the layup encoding, `(Delta_Tf)^2`, and the normalized baseline curvature, `kappa_CLT / Delta_Tf`. It is a fully connected multilayer perceptron with four hidden layers, 256 units per layer, and SiLU activation. The network outputs three nonlinear correction coefficients, `c`, one for each curvature component. The predicted curvature is

`kappa_pred = kappa_CLT + Delta_Tf c`.

This parameterization is physics-structured rather than physics-loss-trained. CLT supplies the analytical linear response, and the network learns only the nonlinear correction observed in the FREEFREE data. In addition, thermal-reversal behavior is enforced by construction: reversing `Delta_Tf` leaves `(Delta_Tf)^2` and `kappa_CLT / Delta_Tf` unchanged, while both the CLT baseline and neural correction reverse sign. Thus, `kappa_pred` is odd in `Delta_Tf`. Matched reversal cases in the solver database satisfied this relation to within `3 x 10^-8` relative error. This approach is distinct from an energy-minimizing Ritz PIML model because no separate equilibrium or energy residual enters the training loss.

### Candidate-ranking procedure and evaluation

For each query, the surrogate was evaluated for every one of the 254 legal canonical ten-ply candidates. Candidate `i` received the component-scaled squared mismatch

`s_i = sum_j [ (kappa_pred,i,j - kappa_meas,j) / d_j ]^2`,

where `d = (0.01, 0.01, 0.02) 1/m` for `(kappa_x, kappa_y, kappa_xy)`. Candidates were sorted by increasing score and the first five were returned.

Performance was evaluated using a random case-level 80/20 split of the 1,024 solver records: 819 records were used for training and 205 for testing. The test partition contained 62 layups, and every test layup was represented in the training set at one or more different temperature fields. The evaluation therefore assesses interpolation of the response of known layups across the sampled thermal conditions; it should not be interpreted as performance for wholly new layup sequences.

## Results and interpretation

Without added measurement noise, the forward surrogate recovered the correct layup at rank one for 204 of 205 test cases (99.51%) and within the top five for all 205 cases (100%) while ranking against all 254 legal candidates. The single non-rank-one result was ranked second. Thus, within the sampled design and thermal domain, the model provided either an exact first choice or a five-candidate shortlist containing the true layup for every held-out case. The prediction-comparison figure reports this unperturbed test set only.

The curvature predictions were also substantially more accurate than the CLT baseline alone. The active-component RMSE was 0.001476 1/m for the neural residual model compared with 0.040236 1/m for CLT, a 27-fold reduction. Componentwise neural-model RMSE values were 0.001588 1/m for `kappa_x`, 0.001472 1/m for `kappa_y`, and 0.001347 1/m for `kappa_xy`; the corresponding CLT values were 0.027982, 0.026562, and 0.052702 1/m. The improvement in twist curvature is particularly important because `kappa_xy` helps distinguish layups containing off-axis plies.

To assess sensitivity to a small measurement perturbation, independent zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.001 1/m was added to each curvature component of every test query. Temperatures, layup labels, and model weights were unchanged. Across 100 seeded Monte-Carlo trials (20,500 noisy queries), mean rank-one recovery was 99.46% with a trial-to-trial standard deviation of 0.45 percentage points (range, 98.54--100.00%); top-five recovery remained 100.00% in every trial and the worst observed true rank was two. Accordingly, the unperturbed result (99.51% rank one; 100% top five) changed negligibly under this small independent curvature noise. These results demonstrate reliable interpolation and rapid candidate screening over the sampled layups and temperature fields. They do not establish identification performance for layup sequences absent from the fitting data, nor do they include experimental uncertainties. For practical deployment, the returned top-five list should be treated as a fast, physics-structured shortlist and checked against measured face temperatures, DIC-derived curvature uncertainty, material-property variability, manufacturing deviations, and actual boundary conditions.

## Computational timing and practical use

With the model weights already loaded, a complete single-model query across all 254 candidates required a mean of 4.54 ms on the project CPU (median 4.56 ms). This time includes CLT evaluation, neural residual prediction, mismatch scoring, sorting, and top-five selection. It is therefore the relevant timing for the layup-ranking portion of a live query, rather than the cost of one neural-network call alone. A three-model ensemble would be expected to require roughly 13--15 ms under the same conditions.

The reported time excludes extracting curvature from a DIC displacement field, reading a measurement file, and initial application startup/model loading. A practical system should keep the selected model in memory and perform curvature fitting upstream of the ranker. Under that configuration, the model-ranking step is effectively instantaneous relative to typical image acquisition, DIC processing, and iterative nonlinear equilibrium calculations; the principal real-world limitation is measurement/model mismatch rather than ranking latency.
